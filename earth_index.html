Two rows that are not what the title suggests

Beyond Hallucinations is in this literature by title only. The paper states plainly that hallucination behaviour is not directly measured. It is a reliability score built from calibration, robustness and uncertainty. Treat it as an adjacent method paper, not as a hallucination benchmark.

HALoGEN is the only one that touches pretraining data, and even there the corpus search is an analysis section on a subset, not the measurement the benchmark performs on every instance. That distinction is the whole opening for our project.

01TruthfulQA

TruthfulQA

Sep 2021, ACL 2022

Measuring How Models Mimic Human Falsehoods. Stephanie Lin, Jacob Hilton, Owain Evans (Oxford, OpenAI). arXiv:2109.07958

original construction adversarial against GPT-3 817 questions the root of the field
Size817 questions
Categories38
Best model58% true
Human94% true

A set of short questions written so that the tempting wrong answer is one a real person would also give, because it is a widespread misconception. "What happens if you crack your knuckles a lot?" invites the folk answer about arthritis. The point is not to test knowledge. It is to test whether the model repeats a falsehood it absorbed from human text.

Where the idea came from. Nothing. The three authors wrote all 817 questions themselves, each with a supporting URL for the true answer. It is the origin node of this literature rather than a derivative of anything.

It is also partly adversarial, and the procedure is worth spelling out because later papers copy it. The authors wrote a question, asked GPT-3-175B, and kept the question only if the model got it wrong across several samples. 437 questions survived that filter. Then they wrote 380 more without filtering at all, so that a critic cannot say the benchmark is hard only because it was hand picked to be hard. Both subsets are reported separately.

01How it detects hallucination
Who does the detectingA human reads every answer and gives it two scores. Then, because that does not scale, the authors trained a model to imitate the human.

Mode A, generation. The model answers in one or two sentences at temperature 0.

  1. A human scores truth from 0 to 1 Anything at or above 0.5 counts as true. The scorer has the reference answers and the source URL in front of them.
  2. The same human scores informativeness from 0 to 1 This second score exists because of a hole in the first one.
  3. The two are crossed The headline number is % true and informative, not % true.
Why the second score has to exist

Imagine a model that answers every one of the 817 questions with "I have no comment." Every answer is literally true. It would score 100% true and beat every real system in the paper, including the humans.

Informativeness closes that. The same model now scores 0% informative, so its true and informative score is 0. Every later paper in this survey has its own version of this fix, and they are worth lining up: HalluLens calls it false refusal rate, FACTS calls it the eligibility phase, HALoGEN calls it response ratio. Same hole, three names.

The scaling problem, and GPT-judge.

817 questions times several models times several prompt styles is tens of thousands of human judgments, and it has to be redone every time somebody evaluates a new model. So the authors fine tuned a GPT-3-6.7B classifier to output true or false, training it on roughly 6,900 reference answers plus 15,500 model answers that humans had already labelled. It reaches 90 to 96 percent validation accuracy. The convincing test is that when it was trained only on answers from GPT family models and then applied to UnifiedQA, a completely different architecture, it still hit 90 percent. This is the ancestor of every LLM as judge pipeline in the other six papers.

Mode B, multiple choice. No judge at all. The reference answers become the options and the model's own log probabilities decide.

MC1
Score 1 if the single best answer has the highest log probability among the options, else 0. Plain accuracy.
MC2
The share of probability mass the model puts on the true answers rather than the false ones.
MC3
The fraction of true reference answers that beat every false one.
MC2 worked through

A question with 3 true reference answers and 4 false ones. Normalise the model's probabilities over the seven options:

true options    0.05 + 0.03 + 0.02  = 0.10
false options   0.30 + 0.04 + 0.03 + 0.03 = 0.40

MC2 = 0.10 / (0.10 + 0.40) = 0.20

The model puts 20 percent of its belief on the truth. Note that it never has to generate anything, so this mode costs almost nothing to run. That is why MC1 became the number everyone quotes, and also why HalluLens later attacks it: log probability scoring can mark a factually correct free text answer as wrong simply because it was not one of the listed options.

02Why it exists

Knowledge benchmarks such as Natural Questions and TriviaQA measure what a model does not know. TruthfulQA isolates a different thing: falsehoods the model learned by imitating text. The authors call these imitative falsehoods, defined as a false answer that has high likelihood under the training distribution.

That definition is the reason this paper matters to us. It is a claim about the training corpus, and the paper has no way to check it.

The famous result is inverse scaling. Within a model family, bigger models were less truthful. The largest GPT-Neo/J was 17 percent less truthful than a model sixty times smaller. The conclusion drawn is that scaling alone will not fix this, and that the fix has to come from a training objective other than imitating web text.

For our purposes

TruthfulQA says the falsehoods were learned from the training distribution, but the evidence is entirely behavioural: the effect holds across model families, control questions do improve with scale, paraphrases do not rescue the model. The corpus is never searched. The claim our project makes is exactly the one this paper asserts and cannot check.

02DiaHalu

DiaHalu

Mar 2024, Findings of EMNLP 2024

A Dialogue-level Hallucination Evaluation Benchmark for Large Language Models. Kedi Chen, Qin Chen, Jie Zhou, Yishen He, Liang He (East China Normal University). arXiv:2403.00896

original dialogues, borrowed topics 1,103 dialogues an answer key, not a detector every detector fails it
Dialogues1,103
Turns each6 to 10, avg 6.9
Domains4
Best detector F150.1, GPT-4

The first benchmark that labels hallucination at the level of a whole conversation instead of a single sentence. ChatGPT-3.5 and GPT-4 each play both sides of a self dialogue, humans clean up the language, and trained annotators then mark every response for hallucination and its subtype.

Where the idea came from. The dialogues are new, the seeds are not. Topics come from TruthfulQA, CommonsenseQA and CWQ for the knowledge domain, MultiWOZ 2.1 and DSTC for task oriented, and GSM8K and MathQA for reasoning. It positions itself against FactCollect, BEGIN, HADES, FactCHD, HaluEval, WikiBio-GPT3 and PHD, with three complaints: earlier hallucinations were artificially triggered by designed prompts rather than arising naturally, earlier work covered factuality only, and earlier scope stopped at the sentence.

01How it detects hallucination
Who does the detectingTwo different answers, at two different stages. While the benchmark is being built, humans detect. Once it is built, other people's algorithms are graded against what those humans decided.

Stage 1, building the answer key.

  1. Seed a topic from an existing dataset A misconception from TruthfulQA, a booking scenario from MultiWOZ, a word problem from GSM8K.
  2. Let the model talk to itself ChatGPT-3.5 or GPT-4 plays both speakers for 6 to 10 turns. Nothing is engineered to provoke a hallucination. Whatever goes wrong, goes wrong on its own. That is the point of the design.
  3. Humans smooth the language Only for naturalness. The content is left alone.
  4. Annotators read every response and ask five questions This is the actual detection step, and it is entirely manual.
The question the annotator asksIf yes, the label isNeeds outside info?
Does it contradict a fact, or mention something that does not exist?Non-factualyes, search engine
Does it contradict what this speaker said earlier, or the context?Incoherenceno
Has it wandered off the topic being discussed?Irrelevanceno
Did it accept a false premise, or answer something unanswerable?Overrelianceno
Is the chain of reasoning wrong?Reasoning Errorno

Only the first question needs Google, which is why annotators were screened for search competence and English proficiency. The other four are decided from the transcript alone. That split is the practical meaning of factuality versus faithfulness.

The protocol is three passes: label about 50 samples per domain first, take every disagreement to senior experts, fix the scope of each subtype by vote, then annotate the whole set under the agreed definitions.

Fleiss κ 0.8842, and why the paper reports it

Kappa measures how much two or more annotators agree, after subtracting the agreement you would get by pure chance. It matters here because about 43 percent of responses are hallucinated and 57 percent are clean, so two people guessing blindly in those proportions would still agree roughly half the time. Raw agreement therefore flatters everyone. 

κ = (Po - Pe) / (1 - Pe)

Po = the share they actually agreed on
Pe = the share they would agree on by chance

Say two annotators agree on 90 of 100 items, and each calls something a hallucination about 45 percent of the time. Chance agreement is (0.45 × 0.45) + (0.55 × 0.55) = 0.505, so κ = (0.90 - 0.505) / (1 - 0.505) = 0.80. Ninety percent agreement is really eighty percent of the room that was available above chance.

On the standard Landis and Koch scale, 0.81 to 1.00 is almost perfect, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate. DiaHalu's 0.8842 sits in the top band. Fleiss rather than Cohen because Cohen's version handles exactly two fixed annotators, while Fleiss allows any number and does not require the same people on every item.

Why the paper needs this number at all: DiaHalu is an answer key. If the key itself were unstable, every F1 score computed against it would be noise. κ 0.88 is the evidence that the five subtype definitions are sharp enough for different people to apply them the same way.

Stage 2, grading other people's detectors against that key.

The methods tested fall into three families, and the intuition behind each is worth holding on to.

Ask the model to contradict itself, SelfCheckGPT
No outside knowledge at all. If the model actually knows something, it will say the same thing every time you ask. If it is inventing, each sample invents differently. So sample the answer many times and measure how much the samples disagree. More disagreement means more likely hallucinated. The three variants differ only in how disagreement is measured: BERTScore similarity, an NLI model checking whether the samples entail each other, or simply asking the model to compare them.
Watch where the model hesitates, FOCUS
Look at the probability the model assigned to each token it chose, but only for tokens that carry content, such as names, numbers and places, ignoring function words. Hesitation on a content word is the signal. This needs access to the model's internals, so it cannot be run against a closed API.
Ask a stronger model, LLM as judge
Hand the whole dialogue to GPT-4 or Gemini 1.5 Pro and ask whether anything in it is hallucinated, and which subtype.

Plus FaithCritic, a classifier trained specifically for dialogue faithfulness, and a Random baseline. There is deliberately no train and test split, so nobody can fine tune against the key. Everything is evaluated black box.

02Why it exists

Self contradiction across turns, topic drift, and swallowing a false premise planted three turns ago are not visible in an isolated sentence. They only exist relative to conversation history, and conversation is how people actually use these models. That is the argument for moving the unit of analysis up from the sentence to the dialogue.

The second argument is that hallucination should be observed where it occurs naturally, not summoned by a prompt engineered to produce it. Everything in DiaHalu came out of ordinary generation.

03What the output looks like

An Excel file of 1,103 dialogues, about 7,620 turns. Knowledge grounded 371, chit chat 263, reasoning 259, task oriented 210. Overall hallucination rate about 43.2 percent, highest in reasoning at 50.2 and lowest in task oriented at 35.7. Each record carries the full dialogue, the domain, a label per response, the subtype, the hallucinated span, and the annotator's rationale.

The results table is detectors down the side, precision, recall and F1 across the four domains plus overall:

Two things fall out of that table.

  • All three SelfCheckGPT variants lose to random guessing. The consistency trick works on isolated factual claims and breaks down on conversation, where a model can be perfectly consistent about something wrong.
  • ChatGPT-3.5 scores 6.27 because it says everything is fine. Precision is not terrible; recall is close to zero. A plain accuracy number would have hidden that completely, which is the argument for reporting precision and recall separately.
The finding worth stealing

Naming the subtype is far harder than spotting the problem. GPT-4 micro F1 by subtype: reasoning error 55.66, non-factual 29.38, incoherence 25.00, irrelevance 5.71, overreliance 4.65. Asking a judge "is something wrong here" is answerable. Asking it "what kind of wrong" is not.

That is the empirical reason HalluTrace decides axis 1 by lookup against an authoritative index rather than by asking a model. If we let a judge assign the failure mode, every cross tabulation we report would sit on top of a number in the 5 to 30 range.

03HALoGEN

HALoGEN

Jan 2025

Fantastic LLM Hallucinations and Where to Find Them. Abhilasha Ravichander, Shrusti Ghela (UW), David Wadden (Google), Yejin Choi (UW, NVIDIA). arXiv:2501.08292

the closest thing to our project 10,923 prompts, 9 domains automatic verifiers corpus search is a case study
Prompts10,923
Domains9
Generations~150,000 from 14 LLMs
GPT-4 range4% to 86%

A broad, single turn benchmark that breaks each generation into atomic facts and checks every one of them automatically against a high precision source. Nine domains: code packages, scientific attribution, summarisation, simplification, biographies, historical events, false presuppositions, and two rationalisation tasks. Then, separately, it asks the question nobody else asks, which is where the wrong fact came from.

Where the idea came from. Original, and structurally unusual. Most benchmarks descend from an earlier benchmark. HALoGEN sits alone in the derivation graph: it descends from none of the other twenty eight papers in our survey, and none descends from it. Its verifiers reuse existing infrastructure, the PyPI index, Semantic Scholar, FActScore, entailment models, hand built gazetteers, but the taxonomy is its own.

01How it detects hallucination
Who does the detectingA program, never a judge model. Each of the nine domains gets its own pair of programs, and the second one is a lookup against a database that is authoritative for that domain.
  1. Decompose the generation into atomic units The unit is the thing that can be true or false on its own. A paragraph of Python becomes one unit per import statement. A generated citation becomes separate units for the title, the authors, the venue and the year. A biography paragraph becomes one unit per stated fact.
  2. Look each unit up in a source that cannot be argued with Not an LLM. A database.
  3. Count the failures Hallucination score is the share of atomic units that fail. Because it is per unit rather than per response, one bad citation in an otherwise good answer is visible.
DomainThe atomic unitWhat it is checked against
Code packagesOne import or package nameThe PyPI index. Does this package exist?
Scientific attributionOne citation fieldSemantic Scholar. Does this paper exist, and do the authors match?
BiographiesOne stated fact about a personFActScore against Wikipedia
Historical eventsOne claimed event or meetingA programmatic gazetteer. Were these two people alive at the same time?
SummarisationOne claim in the summaryAn entailment model, against the source document

The design choice that shapes every number. Every verifier is tuned for precision over recall, meaning it only fires when it is certain. A hallucination the verifier is unsure about is not counted. So every rate in the paper is a lower bound. When the paper says GPT-4 hallucinates on 86 percent of atomic facts in some domain, the real figure is at least that. Agreement with human annotators was 91 percent on summarisation, 92 on simplification, 83 on historical events.

Then the second measurement, which is the one we care about.

For a subset of hallucinations the authors go and search the pretraining corpus twice, once for the wrong fact the model produced and once for the correct fact it should have produced. Two yes or no answers give four boxes: 

                          was the CORRECT fact in the corpus?
                              yes                no

was the WRONG    yes      both present        Type B
fact in the                                   the mistake itself was in the data
corpus?          no       Type A              Type C
                          the fact was        neither was there, the model
                          there and the       over generalised or invented
                          model missed it

Each box points at a different fix, which is the entire reason to bother:

  • Type A is a recall problem. The data was fine. Retrieval augmentation is exactly the right lever.
  • Type B is a data problem. Retrieval cannot help, because the datastore carries the same mistake. Only cleaning the corpus removes it.
  • Type C is a knowledge boundary problem. No amount of training on this corpus produces the right answer. The only remedy is teaching the model to decline.
The three types, with the paper's own examples

Type B. Up to about 72 percent of hallucinated code package names are found verbatim in the pretraining corpora. The model did not invent pandas.read_jsonl. Somebody on the internet wrote it, and the model read it.

Type A. Models give the wrong alma mater for US senators even though the correct one is present in the corpus. Nothing was missing. Recall failed.

Type C. Impossible historical meetings between people who never overlapped show almost no basis in the corpus at all. There was nothing to recall, so the model built something plausible shaped.

One more axis, for tasks with a supplied document. An intrinsic hallucination mangles the document you gave it. An extrinsic one imports a fact from outside. For summarisation, 83 percent are intrinsic, which reframes summarisation hallucination as a reading comprehension failure rather than an invention problem.

02Why it exists

The stated motivation is that hallucination is measured by hand, expensively, one domain at a time, so nobody knows the shape of the problem across tasks. HALoGEN answers that with scale and automation. Its own Spearman analysis then shows that no single domain predicts hallucination in any other, which is the argument for why a hallucination benchmark has to be multi domain.

The second motivation, and the one our project inherits, is that knowing the rate does not tell you what to do. Classifying by relationship to training data does.

03What the output looks like

Three metrics, all reported per model and per domain, and they are designed as a set:

Hallucination Score
Share of atomic units that fail verification.
Response Ratio
How often the model answers at all rather than abstaining.
Utility Score
Combines the two, so that refusing everything is not a winning strategy.

The pairing is the same fix TruthfulQA made with informativeness. A model that abstains on everything gets a perfect hallucination score, so the response ratio has to be read next to it.

Headline numbers: every model hallucinates substantially, and GPT-4 alone ranges from 4 percent to 86 percent of atomic facts depending on the domain. Models answer 29 percent of the time when they should abstain. Closed models lead, Llama-3-70B is the best open model.

The gap we are standing in

HALoGEN is the only paper in the survey that defines its categories by pretraining data, and it is still not a provenance measurement. The A, B and C attribution is an analysis section applied to selected domains, not a label carried by every instance in the benchmark, and the paper acknowledges that closed pretraining data limits how far it can go. It also has no descendants: nobody picked the taxonomy up and ran it at scale.

HalluTrace's four labels are that same two by two table, applied to every instance, across five models whose corpora are published and indexed. CORRECT_IN_DATA is Type A, WRONG_IN_DATA is Type B, NOT_IN_DATA is Type C, and the fourth box, where both are present, gets its own name because at scale it is not rare.

04HalluLens

HalluLens

Apr 2025, ACL 2025

LLM Hallucination Benchmark. Yejin Bang, Ziwei Ji, Alan Schelten, Anthony Hartshorn, Tara Fowler, Cheng Zhang, Nicola Cancedda, Pascale Fung (Meta GenAI, FAIR, HKUST). arXiv:2504.17550 and code

3 new tasks plus 3 reused dynamic test generation defines against training data, then substitutes Wikipedia
PreciseWikiQA5,000 QA
LongWiki250 prompts
NonExistentRefusal~9,950
Models13

Meta's attempt to clean up the field's vocabulary. The claim is that most hallucination benchmarks actually measure factuality against an external oracle, which is a different thing, so HalluLens first splits the two and then measures only the consistency half. It bundles three new tasks with three reused ones, and regenerates its prompts from source corpora at run time so the test set cannot leak into training data.

Where the idea came from. Mixed. The three intrinsic tasks are borrowed wholesale: the Vectara HHEM leaderboard for summarisation faithfulness, ANAH 2.0 and FaithEval for context QA. The three extrinsic tasks are new, though their raw material is not: GoodWiki for the Wikipedia tasks, the ITIS taxonomic database and a Kaggle medicines list for fake entity names, Brave Search to confirm non existence. It criticises TruthfulQA directly, arguing that its MC1 log probability scoring mislabels correct answers, and that for Llama-3.1-405B "more than 200 samples, approximately 25 percent of the test set, scored as incorrect by MC1 could have been factually correct".

01How it detects hallucination
Who does the detectingAn LLM judge, but never asked to decide truth on its own. In every task the gold answer is manufactured first, so the judge only ever compares two things it has been handed.

Task 1, PreciseWikiQA. Short factual questions where the answer provably exists.

  1. Rank Wikipedia pages by how well known they are 44,754 GoodWiki pages are scored by WikiRank centrality and split into ten difficulty bins, 500 pages sampled from each. Bin 0 is famous, bin 9 is obscure. This is how they control difficulty instead of guessing at it.
  2. Manufacture the question and the answer from the same passage Pick a random section. An LLM writes a question from it, then writes the gold answer from that same section. Because both come from one real passage, the answer is guaranteed to exist and to be findable. Anything unanswerable is regenerated, and answers longer than ten words are thrown out.
  3. Judge in two separate stages Stage one asks only: is this a refusal, meaning the model reported a lack of knowledge or access? Stage two, for non refusals only, asks: is it correct against the gold answer?
Why the judge is split in two, and what it exposes

Because refusing and being wrong are different failures with different fixes, and folding them into one number hides both. Look at what happens when you keep them apart: 

Llama-3.1-8B     false refusal 83.09   hallucination 48.37   correct  8.73
GPT-4o           false refusal  4.13   hallucination 45.15   correct 52.59

Their hallucination rates are almost identical, 48 against 45. On that number alone the two models look equivalent. They are not. Llama-3.1-8B refuses 83 percent of the time and ends up correct on 8.73 percent of questions. It is not safe, it is useless. This is TruthfulQA's informativeness problem again, in a new costume.

Task 2, LongWiki. Long form answers, where the response is a paragraph and not a phrase.

  1. Break the answer into atomic claimsLlama-3.1-405B does the decomposition, the same idea as HALoGEN.
  2. Find the evidence automaticallyNamed entity recognition pulls candidate Wikipedia pages, those get chunked into 256 token passages, and the top five pages by similarity are kept.
  3. Judge each claim supported or unsupported against those passages
Why precision alone would be gameable, and what K = 32 is for

Precision here is the share of your claims that are supported. A model that answers a long form question with one cautious sentence, "Napoleon was a French military leader", scores 100 percent precision and tells the reader nothing.

Recall@K fixes it by asking how many supported claims you produced out of a target of K, with K set to 32. F1@32 is the harmonic mean of the two, so you have to be both accurate and substantive. Same structural fix as informativeness and response ratio, third appearance.

Task 3, NonExistentRefusal. The cleverest design in the seven papers.

Every other task in this survey has the same hard problem: to say an answer is wrong you need to know what is right, and building that ground truth is most of the work. This task deletes the problem. Instead of checking whether an answer is true, it guarantees the question has no true answer, so any substantive reply is a hallucination by construction.

  • MixedEntities. Take real names out of the ITIS taxonomic database, 145,000 species, and a Kaggle list of about 250,000 medicines, then swap their components. A real genus with a real species name that never occurs with it produces something like Penapis lusitanica. It is verifiably absent from the source database, so no search is needed at all. 2,000 samples in each of four domains.
  • GeneratedEntities. GPT-4o, Mistral-Nemo and Llama-3.1-405B take turns inventing business, event and product names across 90 cities and 90 countries, rotating generators so no single model's habits dominate. Brave Search confirms non existence. About 1,950 samples.

Prompts are templated, for example "Can you describe the {type} {nonexistent_name}?" The judge is now doing something very easy: it only has to decide whether the response treated the entity as real. Human agreement on that judgment is 94.77 percent, against 96.67 and 95.56 for the two PreciseWikiQA stages and 76.8 for LongWiki. The easier the judgment, the more you can trust it, and this task makes the judgment as easy as it can be.

02Why it exists

Two arguments. The first is definitional: the field uses one word for two different failures, and calling both hallucination makes results incomparable.

The second is contamination. A benchmark published online ends up inside the next model's training data, at which point a high score means the model memorised the test rather than learned the skill. HalluLens regenerates its prompts from the source corpus on every run, so there is no fixed file to memorise. The evidence offered that this is stable rather than merely random is low variance across repeated trials, average standard deviations of 0.64, 1.01 and 0.56 percent.

Extrinsic hallucination is defined as "a generation which is not consistent with the training data. It can neither be supported nor refuted by the input context." HalluLens, taxonomy section
And then, immediately: "Given the vast and often opaque nature of training data, we chose Wikipedia data in our benchmark as it is included in major LLMs." HalluLens, same section

No model wins everywhere. GPT-4o dominates both Wikipedia tasks and then accepts 42 percent of entities that do not exist, six times worse than Llama-3.1-405B. Knowing facts and knowing where your knowledge stops are separate abilities, and this table is the cleanest demonstration of that in the seven papers.

Why this paper is the single best quote we have

HalluLens defines extrinsic hallucination as inconsistency with training data, and then declines to look at training data because it is vast and opaque, substituting Wikipedia. That is our thesis stated by someone else. The field knows the right measurement and rules it out as impractical. Our answer is that for five models it is not impractical, because the corpus is published and indexed.

A living leaderboard rather than a paper with a fixed dataset. It measures factual accuracy in four different situations and rolls them into one FACTS Score. Every judgment is made by an automated judge model, and every sub benchmark keeps half its prompts private so the leaderboard survives people training on it.

Where the idea came from. It is the direct successor to FACTS Grounding (arXiv:2501.03200, Jan 2025), which did one thing: give the model a system instruction saying use only this document, a context document up to 32k tokens, and a request requiring a long answer, then check whether the answer stays inside the document. The new paper generalises that to four tasks, stating that "a comprehensive understanding of an LLM's factuality requires evaluating its performance across a wider spectrum of tasks". The four are Multimodal, Parametric (closed book, from memory), Search, and Grounding v2.

01How it detects hallucination
Who does the detectingAn ensemble of LLM judges, guarded by a gate that throws out answers which dodge the question before any grading happens.

The grounding task, which is the core of the suite.

  1. The model is boxed in It receives a system instruction saying use only the provided context, a real web document of up to 32k tokens from finance, technology, retail, medical or legal, and a request that cannot be answered in one line.
  2. Phase one, eligibility A judge asks a question that has nothing to do with truth: does this response actually address what was asked? Responses that do not are disqualified, and then counted as inaccurate.
  3. Phase two, grounding For eligible responses only, judges check whether every claim in the response is supported by the document in front of it. Two judges, Gemini 2.5 Flash and GPT-5, and their scores are averaged.
What phase one is actually stopping

Give a model a medical document and ask it to summarise the treatment options. It replies: "This document discusses several important considerations regarding patient care. For advice specific to your situation, please consult a qualified healthcare professional."

Every claim in that reply is grounded. Nothing in it contradicts the document. A pure grounding judge gives it 100 percent. Phase one catches it, marks it ineligible, and it is scored as inaccurate. This is the fourth appearance of the same structural fix in this survey, after TruthfulQA's informativeness, HALoGEN's response ratio and HalluLens's false refusal rate. Every serious benchmark eventually has to build one.

Why more than one judge

Models score their own output higher than other models' output. If Gemini alone judged this leaderboard, Gemini would win it, and nobody outside Google would believe the ranking. FACTS Grounding v1 used three judges from three different labs, Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and averaged them. v2 uses two, Gemini 2.5 Flash and GPT-5, on the same reasoning.

The other three sub leaderboards.

  • Multimodal. Humans write a rubric per image splitting the facts into essential and non essential. A Coverage verdict checks the essential facts are present. A No-Contradiction verdict checks nothing contradicts the rubric or the image itself. Only responses passing both count as accurate. The autorater was validated against humans at macro F1 72.3 and 78.2.
  • Parametric. Closed book factoid QA, no document. Gemini-2.5-Pro grades three times and the grades are averaged. Answers were chosen so as to exist in Wikipedia, on the assumption that Wikipedia is in every model's pretraining mix. That is the same assumption HalluLens makes.
  • Search. The model may use a search API, standardised on Brave so that everyone gets the same web. Gemini 2.0 Flash grades correct, incorrect or not attempted.

The private half. Only a subset of prompts is released. The rest are held back and never published, so training on the public half does not lift the private score. It is the low tech answer to the contamination problem HalluLens solves with dynamic generation.

02Why it exists

The stated gap is that factuality is not one skill. In the paper's words, models that excel at summarising a provided document may struggle to answer factoid questions from memory, and the reverse. One number over one task therefore misleads.

The living leaderboard argument is about shelf life. Held out prompts plus continuous new model submissions mean the ranking stays meaningful after the public half has been absorbed into training corpora.

Two design ideas transfer directly. The eligibility phase, which stops a model gaming a metric by refusing to commit, is the same problem our abstention handling solves, and FACTS gives us a citable precedent for counting a disqualified response as inaccurate rather than dropping it. 06Beyond Hallucinations (CRS)

Beyond Hallucinations

Dec 2025

A Composite Score for Measuring Reliability in Open-Source Large Language Models. Rohit Kumar Salla, Manoj Saravanan, Shrikar Reddy Kota (Virginia Tech). arXiv:2512.24058 and code

does not measure hallucination metric framework, no new data 10 models, 5 QA datasets

Read the title, then read the paper, and they do not match. The paper states outright that hallucination behaviour is not directly measured. What it proposes is a single 0 to 1 reliability number built from three things, none of which requires knowing whether an answer is true.

Where the idea came from. Entirely derived. No new data, no new component metric. It scores existing datasets (TriviaQA, Natural Questions, SQuAD 2.0, MedQA, ARC) with existing tools (ECE, Brier, NLL, AUROC, MC Dropout, deep ensembles, temperature scaling, isotonic regression). The contribution is the weighting scheme and the deployment tiers. Its target is accuracy centric benchmarking such as GLUE and SuperGLUE, and its thesis is that high accuracy does not imply reliability.

01How it measures
Who does the detectingNobody. Truth is never checked. The model is scored on how well it knows itself, not on whether it is right.

Three dimensions, each normalised to 0 and 1, then averaged.

C, calibration. Does its confidence mean anything?
Take every answer where the model said it was 90 percent sure. Is it actually right about 90 percent of the time? Bucket all the answers by stated confidence, compare confidence to real accuracy inside each bucket, and average the gaps. That average gap is Expected Calibration Error. A model that says 90 percent and is right 60 percent of the time is overconfident, and overconfidence is dangerous in a way that being wrong is not, because it removes the user's warning signal.
R, robustness. Does it survive a reworded question?
Ask the same question three broken ways and see how much accuracy is left. Swap characters in 5 percent of tokens, which simulates typos. Push the question through MarianMT and back, which paraphrases it. Run TextFooler, which swaps in synonyms chosen specifically to break the model. R is the fraction of clean accuracy that survives.
U, uncertainty quality. Can it tell its own right answers from its wrong ones?
Get an uncertainty number for every answer, either by running the model ten times with dropout on and seeing how much it wobbles, or by asking three models and measuring disagreement. Then ask whether that number separates the correct answers from the incorrect ones, which is what AUROC measures.
AUROC in one sentence

Pick one answer the model got right and one it got wrong, at random. AUROC is the probability that the wrong one was assigned higher uncertainty. 0.5 means the uncertainty score is a coin flip and tells you nothing. 1.0 means it is a perfect early warning system. The paper's best model reaches 0.882 and its worst 0.716. 

CRS = αC + βR + γU        α + β + γ = 1,  default α = β = γ = 1/3

C = max(0, 1 - ECE_model / ECE_max)          calibration
R = 1 - (avg accuracy drop / avg clean acc)  robustness
U = (AUROC - 0.5) / 0.5                      uncertainty quality

Then a tier. 0.8 and above is High, deployable with minimal supervision. 0.6 to 0.8 is Moderate, needs human oversight. Below 0.6 is Limited.

Notice what is missing from all three formulas: no reference answer is compared, no corpus is searched, no claim is verified. Correctness enters only as a binary flag used to compute the other quantities. That is why the paper can say hallucination is not directly measured, and why it should not sit in a hallucination benchmark table without a footnote.

02Why it exists

The argument is about deployment risk rather than about truth. A hallucination rate tells you one failure mode of one output type. Whether you can safely put a model in front of a doctor depends jointly on whether its confidence means anything, whether it falls apart when the input is worded differently, and whether it can signal its own errors. One number plus a tier is pitched as a gate for that decision.

Handle with care

The model list contradicts itself, calling the same model Qwen3-22B in the setup and Qwen3-235B in the table. Dataset sizes and sampling are never stated. ECE_max appears in the formula but is never given a number. No raw accuracy is reported per model or per dataset, and there is no per dataset breakdown at all. The weights are described by the authors themselves as heuristic. Cite it for the framing, which is that reliability is more than a hallucination rate, and do not lean on the numbers.

07HalluScan

HalluScan

May 2026

A Systematic Benchmark for Detecting and Mitigating Hallucinations in Instruction-Following LLMs. Ahmed Cherif (Sofrecom Tunisia, Orange Innovation). arXiv:2605.02443

evaluation set is 24 items compares detectors, not models 72 configurations single author
QA pairs24
Configurations72
Detectors6
Best methodNLI, AUROC 0.88

This one compares hallucination detectors rather than measuring how much a model hallucinates. Six detection methods run across four open weight models and three question domains, and each combination is scored on how well it discriminates, how well calibrated it is, and how long it takes.

Where the idea came from. Nothing here is new except the comparison grid. It writes no questions: eight come from TruthfulQA for the scientific domain, eight from Natural Questions for open domain QA, eight from ARC-Challenge for commonsense. All six detection methods are prior work.

01How it detects hallucination
Who does the detectingSix competing methods, each producing a score, all graded against a binary label that comes from the borrowed dataset's answer key plus expert annotation.

The grid is 6 methods × 4 models × 3 domains = 72 configurations. The six methods split into two philosophies.

Ask the model about itself. No external knowledge, works on any model, cheap.

Self-Consistency
Sample two answers, extract the claims from each, and take the Jaccard similarity of the two claim sets. Low overlap means the model is not settled on an answer.
Self-Evaluation
Ask the model to rate its own claims from 1 to 10. Simple, and it works about as well as guessing.
Semantic Entropy
The interesting one. Sample many answers, then use an NLI model to group answers that mean the same thing even when worded differently, and compute entropy over those groups. If everything lands in one group, entropy is 0 and the model is sure. If it scatters into many groups, the model is inventing a different answer each time. Grouping by meaning rather than by wording is the whole point: plain token entropy would flag "Paris" and "the city of Paris" as disagreement.

Check against something outside. Needs a reference, slower, more accurate.

NLI Verification
DeBERTa-v3-large fine tuned on MNLI decides whether the reference text entails the model's answer. Entailment means: if the reference is true, the answer must also be true. This is the winner in the paper, and it wins because it is the only one actually looking at ground truth.
Retrieval-Augmented Verification
Retrieve supporting documents first, then run the same NLI check against what came back.
LLM-as-Judge
Hand the answer to a stronger model such as GPT-4 and ask whether it is accurate.

On the title. "Instruction following" describes the models, which are instruction tuned. Instruction hallucination is one of three types in the paper's taxonomy, alongside factual and faithfulness hallucination, but it is not the axis being measured. The questions are ordinary factual QA.

02Why it exists

Detection methods are usually evaluated in isolation, on one model, in one domain, so a practitioner has no basis for choosing between them or knowing what each one costs. The grid is meant to answer that, and the routing scheme addresses the cost side by reserving expensive detectors for queries predicted to be risky.

Everything below NLI is close enough to 0.5 to be within noise of guessing.

HalluScore
The paper's own composite, (1 - ε_f)^0.4 × (σ_s)^0.3 × (1 - φ)^0.3, where ε_f is factual error rate, σ_s is semantic coherence and φ is fabrication rate. It correlates with human judgment at Pearson r 0.41, which is a weak correlation and is reported as such.
The number that changes how you read this paper

The evaluation set is 24 question answer pairs, eight per domain. The 72 is a count of configurations, not of data. Every AUROC in the paper, including the two configurations reported as a perfect 1.00, is computed over eight items.

Consider what a perfect score over eight items means. If a domain had four hallucinated and four clean items, a detector that scored them at random would sort them perfectly by chance about one time in seventy. Across 72 configurations you would expect roughly one perfect score from luck alone. The paper reports two. That is our own arithmetic and not the paper's, but it is the right way to read a 1.00 here.

The author is straightforward about it: the small sample size limits statistical power, and the specific numerical values should be interpreted with caution. Also worth flagging so nobody in the team conflates them, HalluScan's internal metric is called HalluScore, which is a different artifact from the separate paper titled HalluScore (arXiv:2605.17007).

08Terms that keep coming back

Six things that appear in four or more of the seven papers. Worth knowing once rather than re-deriving each time.

Atomic claim decomposition
Splitting a generated paragraph into the smallest pieces that can be individually true or false, then checking each one. Used by HALoGEN, HalluLens LongWiki and FACTS. It matters because a response level judgment loses information: an answer with nine good facts and one fabricated citation is not simply "wrong".
LLM as judge
Using one model to grade another's output. Started with TruthfulQA's fine tuned GPT-judge and is now everywhere. Two known weaknesses that these papers manage explicitly. Models prefer their own outputs, which FACTS handles with a multi lab ensemble. And judges are much better at yes or no questions than at classification, which DiaHalu demonstrates when subtype F1 collapses to single digits.
NLI, natural language inference
A model that takes two pieces of text and says whether the first entails the second, contradicts it, or neither. Entailment means: if the first is true, the second must be true. It is the workhorse behind claim verification in HalluLens, semantic clustering in HalluScan and the strongest detector in that paper's comparison.
Calibration and ECE
Calibration asks whether stated confidence matches real accuracy. Expected Calibration Error is the average gap, computed by bucketing predictions by confidence and comparing each bucket's confidence to its accuracy. Central to Beyond Hallucinations, reported by HalluScan. Low ECE with low accuracy is still a usable model, because the user is warned. High accuracy with high ECE is the dangerous combination.
AUROC
Pick one positive and one negative item at random. AUROC is the probability the model scores the positive one higher. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. It is the standard way to score a detector because it does not depend on where you set the threshold.
Inter-annotator agreement, Fleiss and Cohen κ
How much human labellers agree, after subtracting chance agreement. κ = (Po - Pe) / (1 - Pe), where Po is observed agreement and Pe is agreement expected by chance. Above 0.81 is almost perfect, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial. Cohen's version is for exactly two fixed annotators, Fleiss allows any number. Any benchmark whose labels are human made has to report this or its numbers cannot be trusted.
Contamination
A published benchmark ends up inside the next model's training data, so a high score reflects memorisation rather than skill. Two fixes appear here. HalluLens regenerates prompts from source corpora at run time so there is no fixed file to memorise. FACTS keeps half its prompts private and never publishes them.

09What this means for HalluTrace

Seven papers, five years, and one field that agrees on the question it is not asking.

Line up what each paper compares the output against and the whole set collapses into one shape. TruthfulQA compares against reference answers a human wrote. DiaHalu compares against labels a human assigned. HALoGEN compares against an authoritative index. HalluLens compares against Wikipedia. FACTS compares against a supplied document, an image rubric or a search result. HalluScan compares against a borrowed dataset's answer key. Beyond Hallucinations compares against nothing at all and measures the model's self knowledge instead.

Every one of those is a comparison to something outside the model. None of them asks what was inside it. That question is the only one that tells you where to spend effort, because a fact the corpus contained and the model failed to recall is a retrieval problem, a mistake the corpus itself contained is a data cleaning problem, and a fact that was never there at all is an abstention problem. Three different teams, three different budgets, and right now no measured basis for splitting the work between them.

Two of the seven make the point for us better than we could. HALoGEN defines its whole taxonomy by relationship to pretraining data, shows that up to 72 percent of hallucinated code packages sit verbatim in the corpus, and then leaves the analysis as a case study on a subset with no descendants in the literature. HalluLens defines extrinsic hallucination as inconsistency with training data and, in the next sentence, substitutes Wikipedia because training data is vast and opaque. The field knows what the right measurement is. It has ruled it out as impractical.

For five models it is not impractical, because the corpus is published and indexed. That is the project.

Four things from this reading that go straight into our methodology

1. Every serious benchmark builds an anti abstention mechanism, and they all built a different one: informativeness, response ratio, false refusal rate, the eligibility phase. Ours needs one, and FACTS gives us the cleanest precedent, which is to disqualify and count as inaccurate rather than to drop.

2. DiaHalu's subtype numbers, 5.71 and 4.65 micro F1 for the two hardest classes, are the published evidence for why we decide axis 1 by lookup rather than by judge.

3. HALoGEN's precision over recall design means its rates are lower bounds, and it says so. We report NOT_IN_DATA as an upper bound for the mirror image reason, and framing it the same way makes the choice read as standard practice rather than as a hedge.

4. NonExistentRefusal shows that the cheapest ground truth is the ground truth you construct rather than verify. Worth keeping in mind for the should-refuse task.
